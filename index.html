Textbook page 531

Chapter roadmap

This introductory page sets out the questions addressed throughout Chapter 24. The main theme is the distinction between fluid volume, fluid concentration, and electrolyte composition.

Fluid volume

How much extracellular fluid is present? Too little produces volume deficit; excess produces volume expansion.

Fluid concentration

How concentrated is the water-solute mixture? Disorders include hyponatremia and hypernatremia.

Fluid distribution

Why does water move into cells, out of cells, or into interstitial spaces?

Electrolyte imbalances

What happens when potassium, calcium, magnesium or phosphate becomes too low or too high?

Study takeaway: Volume and concentration are different concepts. A patient can have an abnormality of either one or both.

Textbook page 532

Body fluid compartments

Fluid lubricates tissues, dissolves substances needed for metabolism, transports materials and assists temperature regulation.

How total body fluid is distributed in most adults
Exception: infants initially have a greater proportion of extracellular water.

What's in the extracellular compartment?

Interstitial fluid (between cells), vascular fluid (plasma), fluid in connective tissue/bone, and transcellular fluids such as synovial fluid and CSF.

Different fluids contain different electrolytes

Inside cells (ICF)Outside cells (ECF)
Rich in potassium (K⁺)Rich in sodium (Na⁺)
Rich in magnesium (Mg²⁺)Rich in chloride (Cl⁻)
Phosphates and proteinsRich in bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)
Exam distinction: Plasma has considerably more protein than interstitial fluid. The compartments differ in composition, but their total particle concentrations are normally approximately equal.

Total body water depends on age and body fat

Full-term newborn~75% of body weight
Adult man~60%
Adult woman~50%
Older man~50%
Older womanLess than 50%

More adipose tissue generally means a lower proportion of body weight is water. The textbook's 70-kg adult man example has approximately 42 L total body water, comprising 25 L intracellular and 17 L extracellular water.

The four components of fluid homeostasis

Intake→Absorption→Distribution→Excretion

Abnormal fluid losses may also occur. Increased extracellular osmolality is one stimulus for thirst, with further details on the next page.

Textbook page 533

Thirst and fluid distribution

Why do you become thirsty?

ECF becomes concentrated→Hypothalamic osmoreceptors→Thirst→Drink water

Other thirst triggers include reduced circulating volume (baroreceptor and angiotensin II pathways) and dry mucous membranes. Osmoreceptor-related thirst is diminished in older adults.

Filtration across capillaries

Water movement between vascular plasma and interstitial fluid reflects the balance of four forces.

Blue arrow: capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes outward. Green arrow: plasma protein oncotic pressure pulls inward.
ForceTends to move water
Capillary hydrostatic pressureOut of capillary
Interstitial colloid osmotic pressureOut of capillary
Plasma colloid osmotic pressureInto capillary
Interstitial hydrostatic pressureInto capillary (in the textbook model)
Key contrast: Plasma ↔ interstitial distribution is governed by filtration forces; interstitial ↔ intracellular distribution is governed by osmosis.

Absorption

Fluid taken in through the gastrointestinal tract must be absorbed to enter the vascular compartment. Absorption of electrolytes and other particles creates osmotic forces that help water absorption.

Textbook page 534

Osmosis — try the interactive model

Cell membranes have aquaporins that allow water to cross readily, while many electrolytes require transport proteins. When the effective solute concentration differs, water moves by osmosis toward the more concentrated compartment.

High ECF osmolality → water leaves the cell → cell shrinks
Tap the opposite condition. Watch the cell expand or shrink and the water-flow arrows reverse.
Remember: Water travels toward the compartment with higher effective osmolality. High extracellular concentration shrinks cells; low extracellular concentration swells cells.

Normal and abnormal fluid losses

Fluid normally leaves via urine, skin, lungs and feces. Abnormal routes include vomiting, hemorrhage, wound/fistula drainage, GI tubes and paracentesis. Under normal circumstances the kidney is the largest outlet.
